# The Wheel of Emotion

A psychologist named Plutchik came up with the wheel of emotion. Essentially, what he posits is that there are base emotions, that we feel different levels of those base emotions, and that we combine emotions in a way similar to combining colors to get other emotions.

For instance, at the root of annoyance, we have rage. At the root of acceptance, we have admiration. At the root of serenity and joy, we have ecstasy.

As you can see, at the center we have very powerful combinations of emotions. For the most part, there are some negative emotions woven in there. As I said before, anytime we can cause someone to feel a negative emotion, we’re significantly more likely to get them to take an action or respond to our advertising in some way.

# The Psychology of Emotions

Every emotion that a human feels has a cause and effect.

The first thing that happens is that we receive some type of stimulus. That’s the stimulus event.

Then our subconscious—the part of our brain that drives us but that we don’t really control—performs an appraisal of that stimulus event. It decides, “Okay, whoa. What was that? What are we going to do about it? How do we need to feel to deal with that?”

Our subconscious then fires off a subjective reaction. It decides: Is this good? Is this bad? Is it fear? Is it surprise? Should we be sad? How are we supposed to feel?

This is a rote process. We don’t really have control over it. We can control our emotions, but that mostly means controlling how we react to them, not controlling the raw emotion. Our subconscious takes over and fires these things at us. Then we’re left to navigate them and figure out what they are.

After we have our subjective reaction—after we actually feel the emotion—we modify our behavior. Humans feel the emotion, it bubbles up, and we’re like, “Oh my God.” Then we decide to change our behavior based on that emotion.

Our subconscious has then achieved its goal, which is usually a function. We call it a function. It’s a defense mechanism or a different outcome.

Let’s say we’re shopping. We see something, and it lights up like gold. We think, “Oh, I have to have that.”

The cognitive appraisal that happens is that we need to possess it. Then our body feels joy once we start considering the purchase. We think, “Oh my God, this is going to make me feel amazing. This is going to make me look great,” or whatever it is. The emotion starts flowing, and that’s when they’ve got you.

The behavioral reaction is that our prefrontal cortex acts. It decides whether we’re going to rein that emotion in or let ourselves splash around in the joy.

The function of that joy is to say, “Okay, yes. I’m justifying to myself that I need to make this purchase. I’m going to gain a resource or possess an object.”

That’s the actual process we go through when we’re out shopping at the mall or browsing the internet. You see something and think, “I’ve got to have it,” and then you buy it.

An emotion bubbles up and makes us feel differently. If it’s positive, we associate the positive emotion with that object. Then we have to have it. We think the object is actually the result of, or the cause of, the emotion.

Later, when it comes to material items, we learn very quickly that once we get home and that thing ends up in the closet or on a shelf, it doesn’t provide us with joy anymore. That’s because the process caused the joy. The appraisal of the object caused the joy, not the actual object itself.

Once that process runs, it’s rare for it to happen again. That’s why a lot of people constantly have to shop. They constantly want that feeling. They become addicted to it.

That’s a very normal human behavior in our materialistic society. Capitalize on it with your marketing. Create that feeling of joy, and then someone has to possess the object. That’s how you sell a product.

# Stimulus Events

This applies to just about everything, not only possession.

You see someone who is a member of your group, your tribe, or your tribal people. Your subconscious appraises that person as a friend. The emotion that bubbles up is trust: “All right, I can trust that we’re connected.”

That’s why you see people high-fiving and saying, “What’s up, bro?” or whatever. That’s a symbol of trust and tribal nature. It’s a stroking of the ego.

The behavioral reaction is to groom the relationship. That’s why you shake people’s hands or say hello to people you know. If they’re your boy, you do that awkward hug thing that introverts like me can’t seem to master. But it’s cool, right? Trying makes it okay.

The function is to say, “I’m in your tribe. You’re in my tribe. We’re friends now. Whew.”

There are people like me who relate to me, and I’m not alone in this world.

Depending on your product, you may want to create that level of trust. It all depends on the type of product you’re selling and how this process applies. All emotions can be broken down in this way.

Don’t worry. This isn’t the process for writing your advertising. This is just to help you understand how the mind works so that everything will make sense when we put it together.

Far more powerful than the first two examples is a stimulus event involving a threat, such as the sound of glass breaking. You get the fight-or-flight response. Your body tenses up, your shoulders turtle upward, your muscles contract, and you get a shot of adrenaline. You perk up. You’re at peak awareness and peak focus.

That’s your threat drive trying to protect you from lions.

The cognitive appraisal that your subconscious gets from a threatening stimulus event—such as breaking glass, banging, or a gunshot—is danger: “Oh my God, we’re in danger.”

Your amygdala, the subconscious part responsible for your emotions, fires off fear. You think, “Whoa.” That fear consumes your body.

People with a very high level of emotional intelligence may be less susceptible to this, but they’re still susceptible. Fear is incredibly powerful, especially when you layer on things such as family members and the fear of something going wrong with your children.

The behavioral reaction that comes from perking up is escape: “How do I get away from the lion? The lion is going to eat me. I need to run. Do I run? Do I stay? Do I get my gun? Do I fight?”

All these things go through people’s heads when they experience fear. Fear is the most powerful one.

The function of fear is to terrify you into finding safety, returning to your comfort zone, and getting back to a situation where there are no lions around you—such as sitting on the couch, Netflixing in the comfort of your own home, where you’re safe.

That is a very powerful human motivator. You need to understand it if you’re going to work in advertising.

The purpose of advertising is to get large groups of people to take a repeated action. The only way to do that is to trigger an emotion that is common among them, using words and imagery that resonate with large groups of people.

I won’t go through all of these. You can do that on your own. The same applies to anger and disgust. These are really powerful emotions.

Consider a stimulus event involving an unpalatable object. You eat something, hear something, or visualize something that’s bad for you. The appraisal is that there’s poison and we need to get it out of our system. You feel terrible, and the behavioral reaction is to throw up. You eject the poison and get it out of your body.

That can be framed in many different ways. It doesn’t have to be physical poison inside your body. It could be the thought of poison, the thought of being poisoned, or the thought of catching a disease and therefore needing to take preventive care.

This is what all the drug advertisements on television are about. They’re using this type of process to design their advertising.

# Emotions

Different emotions have different combinations of root emotions, similar to how blue and yellow make green when you’re mixing paint.

The same thing happens with emotion. Most emotions we describe so eloquently are simply multiple streams of emotions converging in different ways.

For instance, when we feel love, that’s a combination of joy and trust. Those two things come together, and we love someone. We trust them with our future. They bring us joy, and when we see them, we’re super happy.

There are other combinations. For instance, guilt is joy and fear. When you feel guilty about something, you feel as though you’ve negatively affected someone else. It’s a combination of that fear and joy because you want to feel good about the situation. You feel as though you’ve done something wrong.

That’s really interesting when you think about it. If you know which emotions combine to produce something you want, you can create it through very thoughtful communication. That communication can be verbal, nonverbal, written, imagery, video, music, or a combination of all of the above.

Whenever they’re creating an advertisement, I think a lot of people ask, “How do I sell this person?” They go for the benefits, such as, “Let me lower your interest rates,” or something like that.

That can work. Greed is powerful. In reality, however, the more emotional you can make someone, the more likely they are to take action and change.

Whenever you listen to a really powerful speech delivered by a charismatic person, and it resonates with you because it involves something you believe in—whether religious, political, social, past, present, or something you hope to feel in the future—that person is creating emotions inside you.

They make you feel. When you feel, you never forget.

That’s ultimately what we’re trying to do here: make people feel so that they don’t forget.

Think about something bad that happened to you many years ago. What you remember isn’t necessarily all the details of the event. Most of the memories are gone, distorted, or blurred. If you meditate on it, you can pull some of it back up, but it will still be distorted through the lens with which you see the world today.

What you don’t forget is how you felt.

That’s why negative events are so traumatic for people. That’s why PTSD is such a big problem in the military. These people went through really extreme experiences that triggered endless amounts of fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, and anger. They experienced all those negative emotions at very high levels for extended periods.

The details of the memories fade, but those feelings don’t disappear. When you experience them over and over again, that becomes your norm. That’s what post-traumatic stress actually is.

While that’s an extreme explanation of emotional experiences, it’s very powerful to understand how they work because we can apply them to advertising.

# Target Audience

Whenever we’re copywriting or creating an advertisement, the first thing we obviously need to do is determine who our target audience is.

If you’re talking about insurance or something similar, you need to research online who the decision-makers typically are for your products. Are they young or old? What ethnicity are they? What gender are they? Are they single? Do they have a family?

Realistically, within large groups of people, certain demographic groups are going to have better potential for you.

Yes, I understand that everyone who is 16 years of age or older can have car insurance. That doesn’t help you, and it doesn’t help me. Which demographic group is typically responsible for purchasing the most car insurance?

It’s probably—and I don’t know this for sure—women between 35 and 45 years old who make purchasing decisions for their households. They make insurance decisions for themselves, their husbands, and their teenage children.

It’s that simple. We’re trying to figure out who our prospects are.

In some campaigns, such as plumbing campaigns involving an overflowing sink, it can literally be anyone. However, it will probably be someone who is living independently and owns a home. You’re probably talking about people aged 25 or older, male or female.

If the value of the calls is higher when they own their own homes, you can skew toward an older audience. As someone gets older, they have a higher propensity to own their home.

You have to test, but you need to determine what your audience segments actually are so that you can approach them.

Then you need to put yourself in the mind of the prospect. Who calls for plumbing help? What is that person like at that exact moment?

How do you think that way?

I’m standing in my kitchen right now. I’m visualizing it as I talk. I’m looking out the window and washing some dishes. Suddenly, I reach up to grab the faucet, and it breaks off. Water starts shooting into the air, all over the window and the wall.

I try to stop it. I try to plug it. I grab a towel and try to shove it in there. I’m thinking, “Oh my God, [bleep], water is going everywhere. What am I going to do?”

What am I doing? I’m panicking.

Fear, panic, anxiety, and uncertainty: “Oh my God, [bleep], my house is going to flood. My neighbors below me are going to freak out. I need to call a plumber. I need to call the building. What am I going to do?”

There you have it: panic, fear, uncertainty, and disassociation. Those are the things people are feeling at that exact moment.

We’re going to make a list of emotions prospects should be feeling when they have a problem, are shopping for something, or need something.

We’re trying to target people in a general sense. Obviously, you can call a plumber because you simply want to move your toilet six inches to the left. Maybe our advertisement doesn’t apply to that situation. It won’t apply to every situation. It never will.

That’s why we’re trying to identify common emotional traits among large groups of people. We’re not worried about outliers.

If you’ve run advertisements before, you know that you may get a thousand impressions, and perhaps 12 people click. Maybe 30 click. What did the rest of them do? They didn’t click. They weren’t interested. They didn’t feel it.

We’re not going after everyone. Getting everyone would be incredible, but it probably isn’t going to happen. We’re going after as many people as we can.

We want to create advertisements that have an emotional context. Maybe we try the plumbing advertisement based on panic and it doesn’t work. Then we have to approach audiences for campaigns in as many different ways as possible, using different emotional combinations, and test them.

We create ten different ad groups, ten different images for each, ten different types of advertisements, and ten different emotional traits. Then we run them.

One of them rises to the top. For some reason, people like that advertisement the most. It resonates most strongly with them. They feel it in a way that’s important to them, and that becomes the winner.

It’s all testing. What we’re trying to do is find the most reasonable way to create our advertisements.

Once we have our emotions, we’re going to write words that trigger those emotions. We’ll create a list, and we’re going to focus on words that fit the age range.

If you’re talking to a 13-year-old, they write in a way that isn’t mature. If you’re talking to a 65-year-old, they use a different set of words than I do because different words were popular at different times.

My wife and I are seven years apart. We use different words in different scenarios. I think it’s funny and make fun of her for it, but that’s simply how we are as humans.

Approximately every decade, you’re going to have different words. In some campaigns, that doesn’t apply. You can communicate generically with anyone from any age group.

However, if you’re targeting a specific age group, such as people aged 55 or 65 and older, you should do a little research into the words that were popular at the relevant time.

What will happen is that you can use those words in your advertisements and differentiate yourself from all the other advertisements. An emotion will be triggered that nobody else is able to trigger because there’s some forgotten word that people don’t use in today’s vernacular.

When everyone writing advertisements is a marketing intern who just graduated from college, that sucks. They’re not taking the time to do that research. They’ve never even thought, “The people who are 65 today use different words? You mean my grandmother isn’t responding to ‘savage’?”

You have to understand the mindset and vocabulary of the people you’re talking to. You’re not writing advertisements from your point of view. You’re writing advertisements from their point of view.

That doesn’t mean we’re going to tell people that if they don’t call us, they’re going to jail. This is simply a word exercise so that we can find some root words. We aren’t going to use them all.

What makes people nervous? Panic. When people see other people panic, they become nervous. Deadlines make people nervous.

“Am I going to turn my college paper in on time? I have to pay my mortgage on time.”

A due date is the same thing as a deadline, but they’re different words and phrases, so it’s okay that they have the same takeaway.

Anytime you take something away from someone, or create the fear that something might be taken away, it makes people nervous.

What makes people feel exposed? Faceless collectors. You don’t know who’s coming after you. Your credit score: “Oh my God, someone else is controlling my future.”

Late fees: “They can just bill me. I don’t have any control. I’m exposed.”

What creates vulnerability? “I agreed to something. Oh my God, I have to follow that agreement. I never read it.”

Overdue. Overdraft is another one that isn’t on here.

You can literally create endless lists for these emotions. It just takes a little time.

We also need the other side because we need to communicate that we are the solution.

What represents freedom? Zero dollars due. No debt. Being on time. Options. I can choose my own future.

What creates hope? Reducing. Lowering. Getting relief. Lifting the weight off my shoulders. Becoming debt-free.

What makes someone feel powerful? Fighting. Having lawyers on their side. Being defended. Beating the other party. Winning.

What represents liberation? Being debt-free. Relief. Help. Planning.

What represents being in control? Being debt-free again. Saving money for my future. Investing. Taking back control of my life.

Then we take all these root words and write phrases using them.

# Writing Phrases

I took the words for each of these emotions and put them into sentences that describe a feeling.

For stress:

“Crushing pressure from due dates, late fees, interest, and penalties.”

That’s a trigger sentence. It’s going to cause someone to feel something.

For anxiety:

“Your phone ringing off the hook, knowing you can’t answer.”

They’re anxious and helpless.

That’s going to trigger an emotion.

Their phone is ringing off the hook, and they know they can’t answer. Anyone who is in debt and overdue is being called by collectors. Their phone rings, and the caller is anonymous. Maybe they can’t answer because they’re around friends and family.

They end the call, put the phone back in their pocket, and feel anxious.

We can have someone relive that memory in one short sentence simply by mentioning it.

It won’t work for everybody. That’s why everybody doesn’t click. But this is a common theme and a common memory among people who have debt.

We can trigger that memory and get them to think about their debt.

For helplessness:

“Trapped and alone, drowning in a prison of debt.”

These phrases go all the way. There are no holds barred. We’re trying to trigger an emotion in someone.

Depending on your advertising platform, what’s allowed, how you feel, or how you want to represent your brand, you can soften these things. These are simply the all-the-way-in versions of the phrases.

You can do it that way, too. There’s nothing wrong with that. It may simply be less effective depending on your audience.

You can soften it by removing some of the words. Don’t stuff so many trigger words into the sentence.

For example:

“Are you feeling trapped and alone because of your debt?”

That’s much softer than:

“Trapped and alone, drowning in a prison of debt.”

The second one goes all the way.

I wanted to take the phrases all the way so that you could understand how to practice this. Then you can soften them however you want.

Softer versions may actually convert better depending on the audience. Don’t think that going all the way is always the best approach.

That’s why testing is important. You want to test soft approaches and hard approaches. You want to test different approaches with different audiences so that you can figure out what works best.

No matter how you slice it, this is the process.

For nervousness:

“The feeling of panic from rapidly approaching due dates.”

For feeling exposed:

“Faceless collectors and endless late fees.”

It doesn’t even feel good for me to express this stuff while putting myself in the context of someone who owes money.

For vulnerability:

“Overdue again. You want to pay, but life is out of your control.”

A lot of people who are in debt blame outside influences or outside people. That’s why they’re in debt and struggling in the first place.

We can’t fix that part of it, but we can relate to it in the text by saying, “Life is out of your control.”

People who have a lot of debt typically blame other people. If you don’t have debt, you take responsibility and pay your bills. You figure it out.

People who are in debt, in general—and this is why you have to put yourself in the mindset of your target audience—are blamers.

You can reach that trait by understanding the person and writing copy that fits it.

For fear:

“Threatening lawyers, lawsuits, judgments, and bad credit. When will it end?”

You’ve seen a lot of this in debt-consolidation commercials. None of this is new or groundbreaking as far as the copy itself is concerned.

What matters is that this is a process you can replicate easily, regardless of the type of campaign you’re running.

What do we do with these phrases? They’re really short. Maybe they would work for Google AdWords, but even there you have much more space.

# Solutions

We want to offer people relief and the feeling of these emotions if they call us.

“If you call us, we’ll set you free, baby.”

For freedom:

“Your future is bright, and you have options.”

For courage:

“Fight, win, and take back your life by finally paying off everything.”

When you say that, they realize they’re going to have to pay it off. They’re going to have to deal with it if they want that feeling of courage.

The negative emotions don’t disappear when we talk about positive ones because they’re ten times more powerful.

For hope:

“Reduce your interest, lower your debt, and lift yourself up.”

For happiness:

“Smile and dance while living your new debt-free life.”

For feeling powerful:

“Our expert lawyers will fight on your behalf.”

For liberation:

“Get the help and planning you need to live a debt-free life.”

For control:

“Save your money, invest in your future, and take your life back.”

All I did was take the words that triggered those emotions, toss them into phrases, add a few conjunctions, and turn them into English sentences.

Now we have trigger sentences that will produce emotions in people.

# Combining the Copy and Call to Action

Then we combine these phrases.

In debt campaigns specifically, as well as in many other types of campaigns, we first present the problem and then offer the solution.

I’m going to take two of my phrases from each side, put them into sentence form, and add a call to action.

These are short-form advertisements, so you have to keep them very short. You don’t have much space. It’s totally easy:

“Is the crushing pressure of due dates, late fees, interest, and penalties ruining your life? Your future is bright, and you have options. Call 800-123-4567 now for a free consultation.”

That, ladies and gentlemen, is great copy created through a process that works.

Here’s another one:

“Your phone is ringing off the hook, but you hide the screen, knowing you can’t pay. It’s time to fight, win, and take your life back by finally paying off everything. Call 800-123-4567 now for a free consultation.”

Problem, solution, and call to action, with copy based on the emotions we want people to feel when they read our advertisement so that they take the action we tell them to take.

Combining it for long-form advertisements is the same. You simply take more phrases from both sides and combine them:

“Do you feel trapped and alone, drowning in a prison of debt? Does the feeling of panic from rapidly approaching due dates keep you from living the life you deserve, while faceless collectors and endless late fees suffocate your future?

“There is a way out. Reduce your interest rates, lower your debt, and lift yourself up. In no time, you’ll be smiling and dancing while living your new debt-free life. Our expert lawyers will fight on your behalf.

“Call us now for the relief you need to take your life back. The call and consultation are free. Call 800-123-4567 now.”

The key to a great call to action is telling them what to do and when to do it.

Some people don’t do that. They say, “Call today,” or, “Call us when it’s convenient for you.”

No. I want them to call right now, so tell them to call now. Why delay?

I don’t even want to say the word “delay.” I don’t want to prime their mind with the idea that they can delay, even though I told them to call now.

Say, “Pick up the phone, dial our number, and get out of debt now.”

That, my friends, is the entire process of weaving emotions into copy for successful advertisements. It applies to every advertising platform out there.

# Images

The process works exactly the same way for images.

I’m not going to spend a lot of time on this because it isn’t that complicated.

When I look at this advertisement, I see a camera staring at me. I think, “Why is a camera staring at me? What exactly is going on here? Those apples look delicious. Maybe I forgot my keys.”

I don’t feel anything. I’m confused.

Is this an advertisement for apples? Where did they get those apples? The apples at my grocery store don’t look that delicious.

What do we have on the right?

“Oh, [bleep], someone is breaking into a house.”

It’s that simple.

What’s going on in the image on the right? Someone is breaking into my house.

“Oh, crap. I don’t have an alarm. I don’t have a security camera. My kids may be home.”

That’s it. That’s all you need.

You have to give people imagery that triggers the emotion you want them to feel.

If I’m selling security services, I obviously want fear. That is fear: a faceless person breaking into my house.

You can add some copy to it:

“Are your kids home?”

“Super cameras. Buy now.”

That’s it. That’s how you do it.

If we’re talking about advertising and combining imagery with copy, there’s a very easy way to do it.

Let’s return to the debt-consolidation example and look at it. This is so easy, and the fact that nobody does it blows my mind.

What do we do?

We take the emotions we want people to feel, plug them into Google Images, and receive a list of images showing people in that emotional state.

How hard is this? Why are advertisements so bad? It makes no sense to me.

I’m going to take the copy I just wrote and save almost every image on this page. I probably wouldn’t use the guy with the clocks, but actually, yes, I would totally use the guy with the clocks.

If we’re creating Facebook advertisements, I probably wouldn’t use the laughter image. Actually, I probably would.

I would probably use the Sigourney Weaver image with the alien in it. My God.

If you search for the emotion you want people to feel in Google Images, save almost every image, and split-test your copy with all those different images, you’re probably going to find the one that works best on your first try.

If you’re running a Facebook campaign using images, images are 80 percent of it, and the text is 20 percent. If you use text like the examples I created, you’ll probably win.

If images are 80 percent of it, that means you probably need to test 250 images per advertisement to find the one with the highest click-through rate.

You don’t really have to put that much work into it. You simply search for the emotion, and the answer is given to you.

It’s mind-blowing to me that people don’t do this. It’s because they think advertising isn’t about emotion, but it absolutely, positively is.

Make sure you put yourself in the mindset of the people you’re targeting. Use the emotional wheel I gave you to select the emotions you want people to feel.

Create a word matrix. Write 25 different advertisements. Google the emotion you want people to feel. Put 250 images into Facebook.

There you go. That’s how you make a ton of money with advertising on the internet.
